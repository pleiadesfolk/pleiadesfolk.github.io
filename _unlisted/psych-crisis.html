Pocket crisis plan

This form helps you build a pocket crisis plan on paper, and is an adaptation of an eight-page crisis guide plan by LB Lee.

While it’s possible to save the crisis plan into your Notes app of choice, paper is something that you can carry with you at all times. As LB Lee says in their original guide to Crisis Planning:

Remembering your coping skills in a controlled medical environment doesn't insure remembering outside it. You can hang lists of coping skills on your walls (or make a book of them), but you can't always be home. You can keep your loved ones informed, but you can't always be in their company. Best is something small that you habitually carry at all times--something that's just enough to get you to your books, loves, or doctors.

At the end of this form, you’ll be able to export a typed-up version of your pocket crisis plan, save it, and print it out! This will however be an A4-sized pdf file (sorry Americans!).

We don’t store the PDFs on a server, so you’ll have to save it to your computer or phone when it’s generated.

If you aren't able to export the PDFs, or simply want the zine template, you can find it here.

a) How bad is it?

There's no definitive scale of "how bad things are" — clinicians have tried, and pain scales are often too simple. This one is yours to define.

What are the most prominent signs of your crisis state? When it gets bad, how bad does it get? As LB Lee says: "If you can come up with a simple litmus test that even catastrophically crazy you can get through, you're golden."

It doesn't have to make clinical sense — just personal sense. For reference, here's LB's scale.

c) What should I do when things get really bad?

When you're in crisis, it's going to be hard to know that. Take this chance to tell that version of you what to do — things you can do to cope.

Some examples from LB: HALT (hungry, angry, lonely, tired), take a brisk walk, have a cry, hang out with loved ones, read or watch your favourite recharging media.

You should also list things to avoid — things that might not be obviously bad to the part of you in crisis, like doom-scrolling, calling your ex, or talking to people who've wronged you.

d) What happens if I'm outside and things get bad?

What happens if you've been caught outside? You probably need direction on where to go next, so you don't wander around in a daze.

These places should be where you feel safe — a library, a bookstore, a park, a movie theatre, wherever works for you. Ideally, one of them should be where you live.

e) Who should I call?

Have at least three people you can reach out to. Preferably someone you trust, and someone living in your vicinity if you have local community.

Talk to them before putting them in your plan — let them know what to expect and what you might need. Give them details of the others in the plan, too.

f) What can I say?

Have a script for what you need to say. If you've learned to tell nobody that you're not okay, this is extremely helpful — you need to be able to tell the people around you what's going on, and how they can help.

Write your script using this formula: choose a name from your contacts list, tell them what is happening and that it's an emergency, then tell them what you need them to do.
